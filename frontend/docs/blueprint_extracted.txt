Judge Brief — Read This First The problem Standard navigation optimizes for time, distance, traffic, and convenience. It usually does not explain how route context changes at the time a woman will actually travel: whether lighting evidence is incomplete, whether a segment is isolated, whether help points are nearby, or whether recent independent reports are relevant to that time window. The user A woman travelling through an unfamiliar or late-night route, especially when the journey includes a final walk between a station, stop, cab drop-off, campus, workplace, or home. The solution SaferPath compares route options using  time-aware lighting, activity, isolation, help-point, report, freshness, and confidence signals . It shows the evidence behind the comparison and lets the user choose the trade-off that fits her journey. The hero moment The same route is assessed at 6:00 p.m., 9:00 p.m., and 11:30 p.m. The route context changes only when the underlying time-matched evidence changes. A slightly longer route can become preferable because it has stronger lighting evidence, more active frontage, and verified help points. The MVP The hackathon MVP must demonstrate: origin and destination input; departure or arrival-time selection; two or three route alternatives for one pilot area; segment-level context comparison; visible evidence freshness and confidence; structured route-context reporting; optional, time-limited check-in with a simulated event stream; clear limitations and emergency disclaimers. The demo Enter route → change time → compare options → inspect evidence → select route → start optional check-in. The differentiator The primary innovation is the  Time-Window Safety Lens : the route is not given one static safety label. It is interpreted using the expected time of arrival for each segment, with evidence freshness, coverage, corroboration, and uncertainty exposed to the user. Success metric A user can understand why a route changed, identify the relevant segment and evidence, and make an informed choice without being told that any route is guaranteed safe. What SaferPath does not claim SaferPath does not claim that a location is objectively safe or unsafe. It does not claim to detect crimes, dispatch police, guarantee emergency response, or replace 112, 181, hospitals, police, or official ride-provider safety systems. Executive Summary SaferPath is a  time-aware route-context and journey-support platform . It helps a traveller compare route options using signals that normal navigation often omits: lighting evidence, activity proxies, route isolation, verified help points, time-matched community reports, data freshness, and confidence. The product should be built as a focused CX1002 solution rather than as a generic women’s-safety super-app. The hackathon build must prove one memorable behavior:  the same route can have different context at different times, and the system can explain why . The recommended delivery approach is a responsive PWA backed by a FastAPI modular monolith, PostgreSQL/PostGIS, a transparent rule-based Safety Context Engine, a seeded pilot dataset, and simulated trip events. The first release should not depend on real ride-provider booking APIs, official emergency APIs, nationwide crime data, or an LLM. The business model should begin with sponsored pilots and institutional SaaS for campuses, employers, business parks, mobility programs, and city safety initiatives. SaferPath must not sell individual location histories or allow a paying partner to suppress negative public context. 2. Product Definition and Boundaries 2.1 Product statement SaferPath helps a woman choose and complete an everyday journey with more route context and more control by showing how lighting, isolation, help-point availability, and community reports change with time. 2.2 Core problem The context gap is the difference between  “fastest route”  and  “route I can realistically use with better evidence and more control.”  A route may be technically short but practically uncomfortable because its final segment is poorly lit, isolated, inactive, or unsupported by nearby help points at the expected arrival time. 2.3 Primary job to be done When I need to travel through an unfamiliar or late-night route, help me compare route options using current, time-relevant context so that I can make my own informed decision. 2.4 Product principles Context is time-dependent.  A segment is evaluated for the expected time of arrival, not only for its geometry. Evidence is not certainty.  The UI shows supporting evidence, freshness, coverage, and confidence instead of a guaranteed safety label. Prevention and response are separate.  Route context supports planning; check-in and official handoff support response. The traveller remains in control.  Sharing, tracking, reporting, retention, and escalation require explicit choices. Privacy is a safety feature.  Location, contacts, reports, and evidence are minimised, scoped, protected, and time-limited. Daily usefulness drives adoption.  Route planning and last-mile context must be valuable before an emergency occurs. Missing data is visible.  The system must say “limited data” rather than invent confidence. A modular scope beats a super-app.  Related safety capabilities are extension points, not MVP requirements. 2.5 Non-goals The following are explicitly outside the CX1002 MVP: direct Uber, Ola, or Rapido booking; provider-specific SOS control; official police or ambulance dispatch; a national crime-probability model; continuous background tracking by default; a disguised calculator or deceptive concealment mode; a full complaint-management system; shelter-bed availability; volunteer search coordination; nationwide deployment; an LLM-dependent safety decision; a permanent individual location history; a claim that any route is safe. 3. Functional Requirements FR-01: Route planning — MVP The user can enter an origin and destination, select walking or mixed travel, choose “leave now” or an expected time, and request two or three route options. FR-02: Time-window context — MVP The system calculates the expected arrival time for each route segment and retrieves signals relevant to that time window. The user can change the time and see the route context update. FR-03: Route comparison — MVP Each option displays duration, distance, walking distance, key context bands, caution segments, help points, report freshness, and confidence. The system must not force a single route. FR-04: Explainable evidence — MVP The “Why this route?” view identifies the strongest positive signal, strongest caution signal, unknown or stale data, and the segment to which each statement applies. FR-05: Structured context reporting — MVP The user can report a broken light, isolated stretch, unsafe waiting point, blocked path, help point unavailable, or observed harassment/suspicious behaviour. Reports are coarse-located, time-stamped, rate-limited, and subject to moderation or aggregation rules. FR-06: Optional check-in — Pilot / P1 The user can start a time-limited trip with a planned arrival, selected contact, and sharing scope. The MVP may simulate trip events, but the consent model and event contracts must be designed correctly. FR-07: Trusted-contact view — Pilot / P1 An invited contact can view only the traveller’s approved trip state until the sharing grant expires or is revoked. The default real-time mechanism is SSE, with polling fallback. FR-08: Help nearby — Pilot / P1 The user can discover verified help points with category, opening status, distance, accessibility, last verification, and contact method. FR-09: Emergency handoff — Partner-gated / P1 The UI provides an accurate 112/181/local-support path where applicable. It clearly identifies what SaferPath has and has not confirmed. FR-10: Accessibility — MVP design requirement All safety-critical information has text alternatives, logical focus order, accessible colour contrast, keyboard operation, large targets, reduced-motion support, and no colour-only meaning. FR-11: Privacy controls — MVP design; Pilot implementation The user can understand, grant, revoke, and expire route, trip, contact, report, evidence, notification, and emergency-handoff permissions. FR-12: Moderation and partner operations — Pilot Moderators can review, merge, reject, expire, appeal, and audit reports. Partners can verify help points and publish approved operational updates. 6. UI/UX Specification 8.1 Design language The product should feel calm, precise, and protective without being alarmist. Use warm neutral surfaces, deep ink for trust, mint for stronger contextual support, and coral only for caution or emergency actions. Do not rely on red/green alone. Use a display serif for major product statements, a readable sans-serif for body copy, and a monospaced style for timestamps, freshness, and system status. Evidence should be visually quieter than the decision controls. The user’s choice must remain prominent. 8.2 Component inventory route search field; origin/destination chips; time-window selector; travel-mode selector; route comparison card; context band; evidence chip; freshness label; confidence label; “Why this route?” drawer; segment detail panel; map legend; text route summary; help-point card; report composer; moderation-status card; check-in setup sheet; active-trip status card; discreet prompt; emergency action sheet; trusted-contact permission card; stale/offline banner; privacy and retention control; empty, loading, error, and insufficient-data states; dashboard table, filter, chart, and audit panel. 8.3 Required state matrix Every major screen must define initial, loading, success, no-data, insufficient-data, stale-data, location-denied, network-unavailable, routing-failure, consent-not-granted, permission-revoked, emergency-action, abusive-contact, pending-moderation, rejected, and expired states. Complete User Flows F-01: First-time setup and consent — MVP design User opens SaferPath. Product explains route context and limitations. User selects language and accessibility preferences. User chooses privacy mode. User may add a trusted contact, but may skip it. User grants only route-planning location permission if needed. User reviews emergency region information. User can use route planning without enabling active-trip tracking. F-02: Plan a time-aware route — MVP User enters origin and destination. User selects walking or mixed travel. User chooses leave-now or arrival time. User chooses fastest, better-lit, more populated, lower-transfer, accessible, or lower-cost preference. System returns two or three options. User sees duration, distance, walking duration, context bands, caution segments, help points, freshness, and confidence. User opens “Why this route?” User selects a route or retains the standard route. User can start an optional check-in or simply navigate. F-03: Inspect route evidence — MVP The user opens a route card and sees segment-by-segment evidence: positive evidence; caution evidence; missing or stale evidence; time window; source class; report count and reporter diversity where appropriate; confidence explanation; text alternative to the map. F-04: Submit public route context — MVP User selects “Report context.” User chooses a structured category. System displays the public/private distinction. User selects approximate segment and observed time. User optionally adds a short note or evidence when safe. System rate-limits and validates the submission. Report receives a status and expiry policy. User can later view or request deletion according to policy. F-05: Start optional check-in — Pilot/P1 User taps “Start trip.” User confirms active-trip location consent. User selects expected arrival. User selects a contact and sharing scope or chooses no contact. User chooses manual, timed-prompt, or deviation-prompt mode. System creates a trip session and sharing grant. Traveller sees current status, next check-in, battery/network status, and stop control. F-06: Trusted contact views trip — Pilot/P1 Traveller invites a contact. Contact verifies access. Contact opens an expiring link or authenticated view. Browser connects to the trip SSE endpoint. Server sends the latest permitted state and subsequent event IDs. Contact sees only the approved scope. If SSE fails, the browser uses bounded polling. If the grant expires or is revoked, the stream ends immediately. F-07: Route deviation and soft prompt — Pilot/P1 Event rules detect a deviation or missed check-in. Traveller receives a discreet prompt. Traveller can confirm okay, continue, notify contact, stop trip, find help, or call official emergency services. The system records the response under the active consent version. No emergency claim is made without official confirmation. F-08: Find verified help — Pilot/P1 User taps “Find help nearby.” System shows verified hospitals, police/women’s desks, pharmacies, staffed transit points, partner shops, and other approved points. Each point shows category, distance, hours, accessibility, last verification, and availability confidence. User can call, navigate, share, or dismiss. F-09: Emergency handoff — Partner-gated/P1 Immediate danger presents a prominent 112 path and state-aware support content. SaferPath shows whether it has opened a call/deep link or merely displayed official guidance. It never says “help is on the way” without official confirmation. F-10: Moderate a report — Pilot Moderator opens the queue. System displays category, coarse location, observed time, duplicate clusters, reporter diversity, confidence, and evidence access status. Moderator approves, rejects, merges, requests correction, escalates, or expires. Decision and actor are audited. Public context is aggregated only when quality thresholds are met. F-11: Verify a help point — Pilot Partner or operator opens an asset. Operator confirms category, location, hours, contact, accessibility, and verification source. System records verifier, timestamp, expiry, and evidence. Stale assets are hidden or labelled limited-data. F-12: Privacy request — Pilot User opens privacy centre. User requests export, deletion, correction, or revocation. System verifies the request and creates a  privacy_requests  record. Processing status is visible. Active grants and future processing are revoked where applicable. 10. Real-time delivery decision Use  Server-Sent Events (SSE)  for one-way live trip updates to an authenticated trusted-contact browser. Use REST for commands such as starting a trip, confirming safety, stopping a trip, revoking sharing, and submitting a report. Safety Context Engine 13.1 Separation of concerns The routing provider determines feasible route alternatives. The Safety Context Engine does not replace routing. It enriches route segments and ranks alternatives according to the user’s selected preference. 13.2 Feature dictionary Feature group Example fields User-facing label Lighting coverage, last verified, confidence Lighting evidence Activity open frontage proxy, transit activity, time window Activity nearby Help points count, distance, operating status, verification Help nearby Reports category, recency, reporter diversity, corroboration Recent context reports Isolation segment continuity, inactive frontage, waiting complexity Isolation Infrastructure footpath, crossings, accessibility, blocked path Route continuity Freshness age, expiry, source refresh Data freshness Connectivity GPS/network confidence Trip reliability 13.3 Transparent MVP ranking Use a weighted score only as an internal ranking mechanism and display component evidence instead of exposing one opaque number: context_fit = 0.25 * lighting_evidence + 0.20 * activity_evidence + 0.15 * help_point_access + 0.15 * report_context + 0.10 * route_continuity + 0.10 * transit_or_partner_support + 0.05 * accessibility_fit These weights are prototype assumptions. They must be user-tested and may not be described as crime probability, objective safety, or guaranteed protection. 13.4 Confidence calculation Confidence is based on source quality, freshness, coverage, corroboration, and consistency. A high-volume cluster from one pseudonymous source does not equal high confidence. Missing data lowers confidence. 13.5 Time decay Signal decay is type-specific: road closure: short expiry; streetlight state: medium expiry; help-point verification: longer expiry with scheduled review; community report: freshness decay and corroboration threshold; sensitive incident pattern: careful aggregation, human review, and no permanent street label. 13.6 Context bands Use neutral, explainable labels: stronger contextual support; good context; mixed context; caution segment; limited data; stale evidence; unknown. Never use “safe street,” “unsafe street,” “crime probability,” or “guaranteed route.” 13.7 Example explanation Route B has stronger contextual support at 11:30 p.m. It has two verified help points within 250 metres and stronger lighting evidence. Route A is four minutes faster but includes 700 metres with incomplete lighting data and two independent time-matched reports. Confidence is medium because one lighting source was last verified 12 days ago. 14. Crowdsourced Reporting and Moderation 14.1 Report categories streetlight not working; isolated stretch; unsafe waiting point; blocked or broken path; help point unavailable; harassment or suspicious behaviour observed; road or transport issue; other structured hazard. 14.2 Anti-gaming controls rate-limit submissions; use pseudonymous reporter tokens; require reporter and time diversity for patterns; cluster by canonical segment and time window; downweight duplicate text or evidence; detect rapid coordinated submissions; expire stale signals; provide correction and appeal; send high-impact clusters to human review; never expose exact private incident locations publicly. 14.3 Moderation outcomes A report can be pending, accepted as public context, accepted as restricted evidence, merged, rejected, corrected, expired, or escalated. Every decision includes a reason code and audit record. 14.4 Public output Do not expose “this street is dangerous.” Use a bounded statement: “Elevated caution signal for this segment between 10:30 p.m. and midnight. Evidence: four independent reports across three nights; streetlight data last verified 11 days ago. Confidence: medium.” 15. Security, Privacy, Abuse Prevention, and Compliance 17.1 Threat model The design must defend against abusive partner access, malicious trusted contacts, report brigading, stalking through shared links, sensitive evidence leakage, account takeover, notification exposure, location inference, model overconfidence, false emergency claims, insider misuse, and partner data leakage. 17.2 Required controls short-lived sessions and access tokens; revocable sharing grants; explicit consent records; coarse public locations; encryption in transit and at rest; restricted evidence access; tenant isolation; rate limits and abuse scoring; audit events for sensitive access; deletion and export workflows; data-minimisation defaults; privacy review for new sources; neutral notification language where appropriate; no automatic public disclosure of sensitive incident details. 17.3 Discreet mode boundary A discreet mode may reduce visible notifications and local history, but it cannot guarantee safety. The product must warn that the phone, browser, carrier, cloud account, or another person with device access may still reveal activity. Do not use a deceptive calculator disguise without survivor-safety review. 17.4 Emergency boundaries SaferPath may show official contact options, open a user-requested call/deep link, share a consented location through an approved flow, and display verified support information. It may not claim that police, ambulance, or a partner has accepted a case without confirmation. 18. Operations, Moderation, and Partner Governance 18.1 Named operating owners report moderation owner; help-point verification owner; partner-onboarding owner; emergency-content owner; privacy-request owner; abuse-complaint owner; incident-response owner; data-quality owner; context-rule owner; accessibility-QA owner. 18.2 Standard operating procedures Report review Validate category, segment, time, duplicate cluster, reporter diversity, evidence, and severity. Approve, reject, merge, correct, expire, or escalate using a reason code. High-impact pattern Freeze automatic amplification, notify the safety owner, review corroboration, assess whether public wording could cause harm, and publish only a bounded context statement. Help-point verification Require source, verifier, date, opening status, contact path, accessibility, and expiry. Hide or label stale points. Appeal and correction Provide a correction path for reporters and affected partners. Record the original decision and the appeal outcome without exposing private identities. Privacy incident Contain access, revoke tokens, preserve minimal audit evidence, notify the responsible owner, assess affected data, and follow applicable notification obligations. 18.3 Partner rules Partners may verify assets and consume aggregate insights within their tenant. They may not suppress negative public context, obtain individual location histories without explicit lawful consent, or turn a sponsored listing into an undisclosed route-ranking advantage. 19. Hackathon Demo Script Open SaferPath and state the CX1002 problem in one sentence. Enter a route through the bounded pilot area. Choose 6:00 p.m. and show the route comparison. Show the privacy boundary and explain that emergency services are not replaced. Close with the institutional pilot and revenue model. Judge questions and answers Why not just use Google Maps? SaferPath uses routing as an input but adds time-aware context, evidence freshness, help-point coverage, isolation, and structured community signals that are not presented together in a standard route decision. How do you prevent false reports? Reports are rate-limited, clustered by segment and time, checked for reporter diversity, freshness-weighted, and moderated before becoming stronger public context. A single report does not permanently label a street. Is this a safety score? No. The system uses internal ranking rules but shows evidence dimensions and limitations. It does not produce a crime probability or certify safety. What if there is no data? The UI shows limited data and lowers confidence. It does not infer that the route is safe. How do you get Uber/Ola/Rapido data? The MVP does not assume direct booking or privileged status APIs. It uses fixtures or approved deep links. Direct integration is partner-gated. What happens in an emergency? SaferPath provides an accurate official handoff path and clearly states what it has or has not confirmed. It does not claim that police or ambulance services have accepted a case without confirmation. Who pays? The initial commercial model is sponsored pilots and institutional SaaS for campuses, employers, business parks, and city programs. Personal location histories are not monetised. 24.
